Why Fourier Analysis Works

To ask how much of a frequency f is inside a signal x(t), multiply the signal by a probe cosine and a probe sine at that frequency and measure the net area under each product. When f matches a frequency in x(t), the signal and the probe rise and fall together, the product stays mostly on one side of zero and the area builds up. When it does not, the positive (green) and negative (red) pieces cancel and the net area is close to zero. Plotting the net area against f gives the spectrum.

Try it: with the square wave, move f to 1, 3 and 5 Hz (the odd harmonics), then to 2 Hz or anywhere in between. The area only builds up near the harmonics, so the spectrum is a set of narrow spikes, close to the deltas of a periodic square wave. Then set τ = 0.25 s: at 1 Hz the cosine area vanishes and the sine area takes over, even though that frequency is still in the signal. That is why the Fourier transform uses both, e-j2πft = cos(2πft) - j sin(2πft), and why |X(f)| does not change with the shift. Finally, switch the signal: each shape collects its area at its own set of frequencies.
