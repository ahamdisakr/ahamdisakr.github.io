Sampling and Aliasing

Sampling a signal x(t) every Ts = 1/Fs seconds keeps only the values x[n] = x(nTs). In frequency, sampling makes copies of the spectrum at every multiple of Fs. An ideal reconstruction filter keeps only the band from −Fs/2 to Fs/2. If the signal fits inside that band, it comes back unchanged. If it does not, a copy lands in the band instead and the samples look like a lower frequency: aliasing. The sampling theorem says a sinusoid at f0 is recovered when Fs > 2f0.

Try it: start with f0 = 3 Hz: the samples trace the signal and the reconstruction (blue) lies on top of it. Press Sweep. Once f0 passes Fs/2 = 10 Hz, the reconstruction shows a lower frequency than the signal: in the spectrum, a copy has entered the shaded band. As the sweep goes on, the apparent frequency in the readout falls back to 0 at f0 = Fs = 20 Hz (where every sample has the same value), then climbs again. Then set f0 = 8 Hz, move Fs to its lowest value and press Sweep Fs: the band widens, and the aliasing stops once Fs > 2f0 = 16 Hz.

Predict, then check

With Fs = 20 Hz, a 13 Hz cosine is sampled. What frequency does the reconstruction show?

7 Hz. 13 Hz is 3 Hz above the Nyquist frequency of 10 Hz, so it folds back to 10 − 3 = 7 Hz (equivalently, |13 − 20| = 7).

Choose the sine and set f0 = Fs/2 = 10 Hz. What do the samples look like?

They are all zero: sin(2π·10·n/20) = sin(πn) = 0, so the samples miss the signal entirely. Sampling at exactly twice the frequency is not enough; the theorem needs Fs > 2f0.
