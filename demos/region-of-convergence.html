Region of Convergence

A Laplace or z-transform is not complete without its region of convergence (ROC): the values of s or z where the sum or integral converges. The ROC never contains a pole, so with two poles there are three choices, and the same formula belongs to three different signals. Each pole contributes a right-sided term if the ROC lies outside it (to its right, or outside its circle) and a left-sided term if the ROC lies inside it. The system is causal when the ROC lies outside every pole, and stable when the ROC contains the jω axis, or the unit circle in discrete time.

Try it: with the poles at −1 and −2, choose the ROC to the right of both: x(t) is right-sided and decays, so the system is causal and stable. Choose the other two ROCs: the same formula now gives a two-sided and then a left-sided signal, which grow as t → −∞. Now move one pole to +1: the right-sided choice grows without bound, and the only stable choice is the strip between the poles, which contains the jω axis but gives a two-sided, non-causal signal. Then switch to the z-plane, where the unit circle plays the role of the jω axis. Finally, choose the complex pair and drag it: both poles share a real part (or a radius), so only two ROCs remain, and the signal becomes an oscillation that decays or grows.

Predict, then check

Can a system with a pole at s = 2 be both causal and stable?

No. Causal means the ROC lies to the right of every pole, so it is Re s > 2 or further right, which leaves out the jω axis. It can be stable (with an ROC that includes the axis) only if it gives up causality.

In the z-plane, poles at 0.5 and −0.5. How many ROCs are there?

Two. Both poles lie on the circle |z| = 0.5, so there is no ring between them: the ROC is either |z| > 0.5 (right-sided, and stable since it contains the unit circle) or |z| < 0.5 (left-sided).
