Fourier Transform Properties

Simple changes to a signal change its Fourier transform X(f) in predictable ways. Scaling time by a scales frequency by 1/a, and a = −1 reverses time. A time shift only adds a phase that grows linearly with frequency. Multiplying by a complex exponential shifts the spectrum; multiplying by a cosine (modulation) makes two half-height copies. Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, and differentiation multiplies the spectrum by j2πf, boosting high frequencies. Through all of them, Parseval’s theorem says the energy is the same in both domains.

Try it: with the rectangular pulse and time scaling, raise a to 2: the pulse becomes half as wide and its spectrum twice as wide and half as tall. Move a to −1 with the exponential: the signal is reversed, |Y(f)| stays the same and the phase flips sign. Switch to time shift and press Play: |Y(f)| does not move, while the phase becomes a steeper and steeper ramp. Compare frequency shift with modulation: the complex exponential moves the whole spectrum to fc, while the cosine, half of ej2πfct plus half of e−j2πfct, makes two half-height copies. With convolution, widen the averaging pulse: y(t) gets smoother and its spectrum narrower. Finally, differentiate the triangle: the result is two rectangular pulses, and the spectrum is boosted at high frequencies. Watch the two energies in the Parseval readout stay equal throughout. To look at the side lobes or the phase closely, zoom into the spectrum plots (Ctrl + scroll or pinch, drag to pan, double-click to reset).

Predict, then check

The rectangular pulse (1 s wide) has its first spectral zeros at ±1 Hz. With a = 4, where are the first zeros, and how tall is |Y(0)|?

At ±4 Hz, and |Y(0)| = 1/4. The pulse is 4 times shorter, so its spectrum is 4 times wider and, since Y(0) is the area under y(t), 4 times lower.

Shift the Gaussian pulse by t0 = 1 s. Does |Y(f)| change? What is the phase at f = 0.25 Hz?

|Y(f)| does not change. The phase is −2πft0 = −2π(0.25)(1) = −π/2, that is −90°.

Convolve the rectangular pulse with an averaging pulse of the same width, W = 1 s. What shape is y(t), and what is Y(f)?

A triangle from −1 to 1 s with peak 1, the triangular pulse. Its transform is the product of the two spectra, sinc(f)·sinc(f) = sinc²(f).
